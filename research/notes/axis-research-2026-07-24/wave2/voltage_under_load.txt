Register allocation is graph coloring; that part you can read anywhere. What decides whether a compiler is good is what happens when coloring fails and you must choose a value to spill. The obvious heuristic, spill the longest live range, is wrong inside loops: a range spanning a loop body pays its reload once per iteration, not once. So the cost model must weight references by loop depth, and loop depth depends on the schedule you have not built yet. That circularity is the actual problem.

Ice sheet models are boring in the interior, where the flow law does what the flow law says. Everything difficult happens at the grounding line, the moving boundary where ice starts to float. There the stress balance changes character within a few kilometers, and a model whose grid is coarser than that transition will report a stable margin no matter how the ocean warms. Refining the grid is not enough, because the boundary migrates between timesteps; the discretization has to track it, or you are measuring your mesh instead of the ice.

Protein structure prediction is largely solved for the easy cases, meaning single domains with deep homology. The residual difficulty is disordered regions that fold only on binding. There the target is not a structure but a conditional ensemble, and the standard accuracy metric assumes a single reference conformation, so a model that correctly predicts a two-state distribution scores worse than one that confidently predicts the wrong single state. The benchmark penalizes the right answer, so progress here cannot be read off the leaderboard.
